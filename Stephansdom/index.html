Background

A church that grew for centuries

The Stephansdom was not built in one go. It grew piece by piece over hundreds of years, and you can still read that history in its stones: Romanesque at the front, Gothic in the middle, and a Renaissance cap on the unfinished north tower.

The first church on the site was consecrated in 1147. Its west front, with the Giant Door and the two Heathen Towers, was rebuilt in Romanesque style between 1230 and 1245 and still stands today. In the 1300s the Habsburg rulers began turning it into a huge Gothic church: the Albertine choir was consecrated in 1340, and Duke Rudolf IV laid the foundation stone for the Gothic nave and south tower in 1359.

The south tower took from 1368 to 1433 to build. At 136.44 m it was one of the tallest church towers of its time. The north tower was meant to match it, but work stopped in 1511 at about half the height, and in 1578 it was finished with the rounded Renaissance cap you will build at the end.

Reading the architecture

Look at your finished model and you can find each period of the building:

Romanesque west front

The oldest part: the Giant Door (W02) and the two Heathen Towers (W01, W03, W04 with their octagonal caps H01 and J01).

Gothic nave and choir

The long hall with tall windows and stone buttresses (P01–P14) pushing against the walls to carry the weight of the vaults.

The square-to-octagon south tower

The Steffl starts as a square box (T01–T04) and turns into a tapering octagonal spire (S02–S13) ending in a needle and cross.

The tiled roof and the eagle

The steep roof is covered with patterned glazed tiles. On the south side of the choir roof the tiles form a double-headed eagle, engraved on your roof panel R03.

Nearly every Viennese generation since the 1100s added something, and the city rebuilt it together after 1945.

Why it matters

The Stephansdom stands at Stephansplatz, the centre of Vienna, and its south tower is the city’s best-known landmark. It is the seat of the Archbishop of Vienna and has seen the city’s great moments, from imperial funerals to Mozart’s wedding.

It also stands for recovery. When the roof burned in 1945, people across Austria helped rebuild it, and the new Pummerin bell was cast in Upper Austria. Today it is a must-see for nearly every visitor to Vienna.

From 136 metres to 103 millimetres

Your model is about 1 : 1379 of the real cathedral. Making it from flat 3 mm plywood without glue meant a few honest compromises:

Roof ridgesThe south roof panels overlap the north ones. From the north you can see a narrow shadow line under the overlap.
Apse cornersThe corners are square-cut, so there are hairline gaps where the real walls meet at an angle.
Roof earsThe tabs that hold the roofs show as small flush squares in the tiles.
West towersThe caps sit about 2 mm towards the nave, because their spines are the tower back walls.
Spire tipThe south spire ends in a 3 mm needle above the last layer instead of several tiny layers.
